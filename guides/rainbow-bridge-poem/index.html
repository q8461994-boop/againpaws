The Rainbow Bridge Poem: Meaning, Origin and Memorial Ideas

By the Again Paws studio · November 2026

At some point in every pet loss conversation, someone mentions a meadow, a rainbow, and a healthy old dog running again. The Rainbow Bridge poem has become the shared language of pet grief — printed on cards, read at farewells, engraved beside photos. Here is what it actually says, where it came from, and what to do with it.

What the Rainbow Bridge poem says

The poem describes a place "just this side of heaven": a warm meadow where pets go after they die. There, restored to health and youth — the old dog runs, the blind cat sees — they play together until each one is whole again. Then they wait, by a rainbow bridge, for the person they spent their life with. When that person finally arrives, they cross the bridge together, into heaven, never to be parted.

“Just this side of heaven is a place called Rainbow Bridge…
When an animal dies that has been especially close to someone here, that pet goes to Rainbow Bridge.” — opening lines of the traditional text (author unknown; widely circulated since the 1980s–90s)

That is the whole arc: healing, waiting, reunion. No sin, no judgment, no doctrine — just a reunion you do not have to be religious to want.

Who wrote it? An honest answer: nobody knows

The poem spread anonymously through newsgroups, newsletters and photocopied cards from the late 1980s onward. Authorship is genuinely disputed: a 1959 version is attributed to Edna Clyne-Rekhy, a Scottish-born writer living in the Netherlands; Paul C. Dahm published a book using the phrase Rainbow Bridge in 1981 and claimed the concept. Several others have stepped forward too. Courts and researchers have not settled it, and the version most people share is the anonymous one. When someone asks, the honest answer is: it appeared in the pet loss community decades ago, and its author is unknown.

Why it became the poem of pet grief

  • It promises restoration, not just remembrance. The pet is not a memory in the meadow — it is healed, young and playing. For anyone who watched a pet suffer, that detail matters more than any theology.
  • It waits for you. Most afterlife imagery is one-directional. Rainbow Bridge makes the owner part of the story — the pet is not just at peace, it is waiting for you.
  • It fits every faith and none. Heaven-adjacent but doctrine-free, it can be read at a secular farewell or a religious one without friction.

Ways people use the Rainbow Bridge poem

  • Printed alongside a photo. The most common use — a framed photo of the pet with a few lines of the poem beneath it, on a memorial shelf or beside an urn.
  • Engraved on a memorial stone. A short excerpt under the pet's name works well on a garden stone with a photo window, marking a grave or favorite spot.
  • Read at a farewell. For families who hold a small goodbye ceremony — burial, cremation or a farewell offering set — the poem gives the moment words when nobody has any.
  • In a sympathy card. If you are sending support to someone else, a couple of lines of the poem above a short personal note is the classic pairing — our guides to what to say and sympathy gift etiquette cover the rest.
  • Beside a portrait of the pet. Many families pair the poem with a likeness of the pet itself — a handmade felted portrait from their photos turns "running healthy at the bridge" into something you can actually see.

If the poem is not for you

Some people find the poem too sweet, or simply worn out from repetition. That is a legitimate reaction, not a failure of grief. Alternatives that land well: a short quotation of your own about the pet, the pet's name and dates engraved simply, or a quiet ritual with no words at all — a walk, a planted tree, a photo on the shelf. Frequently asked

What is the Rainbow Bridge poem?

A short prose poem about a meadow just outside heaven where pets go after death. They run and play until healed, then wait beside a rainbow bridge for their owner, and cross it together when the owner finally arrives.

Who wrote the Rainbow Bridge poem?

No one knows for certain. It circulated anonymously from the late 1980s and early 1990s; authorship is claimed both by Edna Clyne-Rekhy (a 1959 version) and Paul C. Dahm (1981), and has never been settled.

Is the Rainbow Bridge a religious concept?

Not exactly. It borrows imagery from heaven but belongs to no faith — which is a large part of its appeal: comfort without doctrine.

Can I print the poem on a memorial gift?

The traditional text has circulated anonymously for decades and is widely reproduced for personal use. For anything commercial, check the specific version you are copying and its stated copyright.
