What to Do With Pet Ashes: 9 Meaningful Options

By the Again Paws studio · November 2026

The vet hands you a small box, and suddenly you are supposed to make a decision about the rest of forever. Nobody prepares you for this part — the practical question sitting underneath the grief: what do I actually do with the ashes? There is no single right answer. There are nine common ones, and the right choice depends on where you live, how you grieve, and whether you want a place to visit or something to hold.

First, know what you are holding

Cremation returns roughly 3–5% of body weight. A 10 lb cat leaves about half a cup; a 50 lb dog leaves two to three cups. That matters for two reasons: a large dog's ashes will not fit in a small decorative urn, and there is usually enough to divide between family members if you want to.

If you have not collected the ashes yet, ask the crematorium two questions: whether the cremation was individual (only your pet) or communal, and whether they will divide the ashes into separate containers for you. Doing it yourself is possible but messy; most facilities will do it on request.

1. Keep them at home in an urn

The simplest and most common choice — and there is nothing morbid about it. A custom cremation urn with your pet's portrait ($28.50 in four colors) turns a plain box into something you are glad to see on the shelf. Place it somewhere you actually pass every day, not in a cupboard: the point is presence, not storage.

2. Build a small memorial shelf or corner

An urn, a framed photo, their collar, a candle. This is the "place to visit" that apartments can offer when there is no garden. Keep it dusted and lit occasionally — a ritual that takes ten seconds and does more than it sounds like it should.

3. Bury them in the garden

If you have soil, this is the most grounded option, literally. Practical points: dig about two to three feet deep, avoid spots near water lines or property boundaries, and check local rules first — many municipalities regulate backyard pet burial, especially after a contagious illness. Mark the spot with a weather-safe memorial garden stone so it reads as a grave and not a patch of disturbed earth.

4. Scatter them somewhere they loved

A favorite trail, the beach they ran on, a lake they swam in. Two honest warnings: public land — parks, beaches, trails, waterways — usually has rules, and some places prohibit scattering remains entirely. Private land with the owner's permission is the safest bet. And scattering is irreversible: if you are unsure, keep a portion and scatter the rest later.

5. Plant a tree or shrub with them

A living marker beats a static one for many people. One correction to a common belief: cremation ash is alkaline and nutrient-poor, so it does not "feed" a plant. Mixed sparingly into the backfill around a hardy native tree, it does no harm; treat the planting as ritual, not fertilizer. Do not dump a whole container at the roots.

6. Keep a portion in jewelry or a keepsake

Pendants, rings and small vessels hold a teaspoon or two — enough for the people who want their pet with them rather than on a shelf. If several family members want a portion, this is the cleanest way to share.

7. Split them between family members

Adult children, ex-partners, the person who actually did the 6am walks. Ashes can be divided; ask the crematorium to do it evenly, and give each person a small vessel. Emotional logistics matter more than the physical ones here — decide together, ideally before anyone feels left out.

8. Choose a pet cemetery or columbarium

Some countries have dedicated pet cemeteries with plots and niches; a few allow interment alongside human family members, though rules vary widely. It is the most formal option, and the most expensive — but it is a real answer for people who want an official, visitable resting place.

9. Wait

Genuinely an option. Ashes do not expire, and no decision has to be made in the first month. Many people keep the box on a shelf for a year and then know exactly what they want. Delay is not avoidance; it is letting the answer arrive instead of forcing it.

How to choose

Ask one question: do you want a place to visit, or something to hold? Burying, scattering, a cemetery plot and a garden stone serve the first. An urn at home, jewelry and a keepsake serve the second. Families often end up with both — the urn inside, the stone in the garden. Frequently asked

How much ash does a pet leave behind?

Roughly 3–5% of body weight. A 10 lb cat leaves about half a cup; a 50 lb dog leaves two to three cups. That is why a single small urn often cannot hold a large dog's ashes.

Can I split my pet's ashes between family members?

Yes. Many families divide ashes into several smaller urns or keepsake vessels so each person has a portion. Ask the crematorium to do the division if you would rather not handle it yourself.

Is it legal to bury my pet in the backyard?

In many places yes, but rules vary by country, state and municipality — usually covering depth (often 2–3 feet), distance from water sources and property lines, and whether the pet died of a contagious disease. Check local regulations before digging.

Can I scatter pet ashes anywhere?

Not anywhere. Public parks, beaches, trails and waterways usually have rules, and some places prohibit scattering remains. Private land with permission is the safest option.

Do pet ashes help plants grow?

Not directly — cremation ash is alkaline and nutrient-poor. Mixed sparingly into soil it does no harm, but treat it as ritual, not fertilizer.
